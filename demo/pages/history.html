Reading: The Treaty of Versailles (1919)

The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles, formally ending the state of war between Germany and the Allied Powers. Its terms were drafted largely by the victorious nations, principally France, Britain, the United States and Italy, while Germany was given little room to negotiate. The treaty also created the League of Nations and redrew the borders of much of Europe.

Article 231, often called the 'war guilt clause', required Germany to accept responsibility for the loss and damage caused by the war. Germany was also required to pay reparations for damage to Allied civilians and their property. The total was not fixed until 1921, when it was set at 132 billion gold marks. Payments were later reduced and rescheduled during the 1920s, but reparations remained a contested issue in German politics.

The treaty also stripped Germany of about 13 per cent of its European territory and roughly 10 per cent of its population, including Alsace-Lorraine, which returned to France. Its military terms capped the army at 100,000 men, abolished conscription, and barred submarines and military aircraft. Many Germans viewed the treaty as a 'Diktat', a peace imposed under threat rather than negotiated, and nationalist politicians later used this grievance against the Weimar Republic.

Discussion question: Why did many Germans see the treaty as a 'Diktat'?
